Across, Then Up

Two numbers name one place. The order is not negotiable.
NAME ___________
DATE ___________
✦
Warm-Up
Name the across number
(3, 5)
(7, 1)
(0, 6)
(4, 0)
(2, 9)
(6, 6)
(3, 5) and (5, 3) are two different rooms
(3,5)
(5,3)
Both points use a 3 and a 5, and they are nowhere near each other.

The first number is always how far across. The second is always how far up. Say it out loud as you plot — “three across, five up” — and the mistake becomes impossible to make.

The origin, where the two lines meet, is (0, 0): no distance across, no distance up.

How Far Apart

Same row or same column? Subtract. Otherwise, go across then up.
NAME ___________
DATE ___________
✦
Warm-Up
Distances along a line
(2,3)→(7,3)
(4,1)→(4,8)
(0,0)→(6,0)
(1,2)→(1,9)
(3,5)→(9,5)
(5,2)→(5,2)
(2,3) to (8,7) · the route is 10, the gap is not
6 across
4 up
Going across and up, the journey is 6 + 4 = 10 units. That is the distance if you have to follow streets.

The straight-line gap is shorter than 10 — you can see that on the grid. Finding it exactly needs maths you will meet in a couple of years, so this year we always say which kind of distance we mean.

When both points share a row or a column, there is no difference: just subtract.

Four corners, and everything follows
Corners at (1,1), (6,1), (6,5) and (1,5).

Width: 6 − 1 = 5.
Height: 5 − 1 = 4.
Area: 5 × 4 = 20.
Perimeter: 5 + 4 + 5 + 4 = 18.

No measuring, no ruler — just subtraction on the coordinates.

Directions Somebody Can Follow

Write a route. Hand it over. Ambiguity is the enemy.
NAME ___________
DATE ___________
✦
Warm-Up
One move at a time
(0,0) move 3 across
(3,0) move 2 up
(5,5) move 1 across
(2,2) move 2 up
blocks (0,0)→(4,0)
blocks (0,0)→(0,7)
Two sets of directions to the same place
Useless
“Go along a bit, then head up past the park until you get near the school.”
Usable
“Start at (1,1). Go 6 across to (7,1). Go 5 up to (7,6). Total: 11 blocks.”
Coordinates exist because words like “a bit” and “near” cannot be followed by somebody who is not already there. Today's test is whether your directions work when somebody else reads them.
◆
Core
1.  (1,2) to (6,2) then 3 up — the end point
2.  Total blocks on that route
3.  (1,1) to (7,1) to (7,6) — total blocks
4.  Shortest across-and-up route (2,3) to (9,8)
5.  Write directions from (0,0) to (5,4) using only coordinates and numbers of blocks. Give them to somebody and see if they land in the right place.
★
Challenge

C2. How many different shortest across-and-up routes go from (0,0) to (2,2)? Draw them all.
