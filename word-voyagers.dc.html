Checking the notebook

Every lesson makes one notebook entry of six numbered tasks, written in order across the lesson.

Task 2
The day's quote, copied word for word, exactly as it is written.
Task 3
Where the quote came from, to give proper credit to the author.
Task 4
What the quote's translation means to him, in one or two sentences.
Task 5
The challenge sentence he chose, copied exactly. The correct choice is shown below for each day.
Task 6
His answer to the critical-thinking question.

Tasks 2, 3 and 5 are copying, so they are either exact or they are not — check them against the screen. Tasks 4 and 6 are his thinking, and there is no single right answer; what you are looking for is whether he has used the text rather than his own opinion of it.

How the days are shaped

Nine unit studies, four weeks each: 36 weeks, 180 lessons. Every week runs the same five-lesson rhythm, and each lesson is one screen your child can start alone: a reading lesson, a grammar lesson, a spelling lesson, a handwriting lesson, then a speaking and review lesson.

Grammar, spelling and reading questions grade themselves instantly with an explanation. The handwriting lesson's page is marked by you on paper. The speaking lesson's task is deliberately NOT machine-graded: there is no honest way to score a child reporting aloud or building on someone's idea in a discussion, so the page gives the task and the rubric and your child marks it done with whoever they did it with. That is the one place in the week that genuinely needs another person.

Catching up and working ahead

Nothing is locked anywhere in this curriculum. Every day and every step is open, in any order, but a drill only ticks its step off at 80% or better: under that, it offers another round built from what was missed, as many rounds as it takes. After three short rounds it is also flagged for you below, and you can pass the step yourself if something is genuinely wrong. What the app does instead is tell him: a day with unfinished work behind it opens with a warning naming that day and a button back to it, and a short score offers another round rather than demanding one. He can also work as far ahead as he likes.

The trade is deliberate and yours to reverse: he cannot be walled out of school by a bad morning, and he also cannot be stopped from skipping something. The two lists below are how you see what actually happened.

Excuse a day

If a day is genuinely never going to be made up — illness, a family week, or you started partway through the year — excuse it here. An excused day counts as finished for ordering only, so the rest of the year opens. Without this, one lost week would wall your child out of everything after it, and a rule with no release valve is a trap rather than a rule.
